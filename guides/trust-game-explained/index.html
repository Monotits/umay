The Trust Game Explained: How Much Would You Send?

By Buğra Günay, maker of MindType

How the Trust (investment) game works, what economists expected, what Berg, Dickhaut and McCabe found, and what sending and returning say about trust and reciprocity. Play it on iPhone with MindType.

The Trust game, also called the investment game, measures how much people trust strangers and how much they repay trust. It was popularised by Joyce Berg, John Dickhaut and Kevin McCabe in 1995.

How it works

  1. The investor receives an amount, say $10, and chooses how much to send to a trustee.
  2. Whatever is sent is tripled.
  3. The trustee decides how much of the tripled amount to send back.

If the investor sends $10, the trustee receives $30 and could return $15, leaving both better off than if nothing had been sent.

The prediction and the reality

If everyone only maximises money, the trustee returns nothing, so the investor should send nothing. In experiments, most investors send something, often around half, and many trustees return enough to make trust pay, though returns vary widely.

What it measures

  • Sending measures trust: your willingness to take a risk on someone you do not know.
  • Returning measures reciprocity: whether you repay trust when you could keep everything.

Why it matters

Trust lowers the cost of cooperation in teams, markets and friendships. People and societies with higher trust can do more together, while misplaced trust can be exploited. Noticing your own default helps you decide when to extend trust and when to verify first.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Trust game in economics?

A two-player game where an investor sends money to a trustee, the amount is multiplied, and the trustee decides how much to return.

Who created the Trust game?

It was introduced in a 1995 study by Joyce Berg, John Dickhaut and Kevin McCabe.

What does the Trust game measure?

How much people trust strangers (what they send) and how much they reciprocate (what they return).
